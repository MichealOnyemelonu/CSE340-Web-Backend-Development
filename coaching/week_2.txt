Copilot said: That's a thoughtful answer, Micheal.
What you've described is actually a common design concern: keeping data organized and avoiding putting unrelated or repeating information into the same table. The risk wasn't so much that the database would "crash," but that the design would become harder to manage.
For example, if service_project had only one category_id, each project could belong to only one category. If a project needed both "Environment" and "Community Service" categories, you'd have a problem. The project_category junction table solves that by allowing many categories per project and many projects per category.
So your reasoning could be refined to:

"I used a separate junction table to keep the data normalized and to support a many-to-many relationship between projects and categories."

That's a strong database-design explanation.
